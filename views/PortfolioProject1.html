Predicting Next-Day Stock Direction

I chose historical daily S&P 500 stock data because I am interested in quantitative finance. Each row contains a stock's date, open, high, low, close, volume, and ticker. I wanted to test whether recent trading behavior helps predict whether the next trading day's close is higher.

Source: Cam Nugent's S&P 500 stock data, all_stocks_5yr.csv. Target: 1 = higher next close; 0 = lower or unchanged. Predictions use information available after today's close.

619,040 rows | 505 tickers | Feb 08, 2013 - Feb 07, 2018

1. Feature engineering and missing values

I used returns and ratios instead of raw prices so different stocks are easier to compare. Missing prices were not filled with averages. I kept missing dates in place while calculating features, then removed examples without enough valid history or a known next close.

Missing original records: 11 | Inconsistent records: 12
Missing trading-day slots retained during calculations: 51
Usable labeled observations: 608,193 | Predictors: 14

The predictors cover momentum (1-, 5-, and 10-day returns, RSI, relative price), daily behavior (range, opening gap, close position), activity (volume change and relative volume), volatility, and market-wide movement. A return of 0.02 means 2%; a volume ratio of 1 means the 20-day average.

2. Text fields and correlations

Name identifies the stock and is used for grouping, not prediction. Tickers have no meaningful order. Since I kept only numerical predictors, no ordinal or one-hot encoding was needed. date is used for sorting and splitting, not as an input.

I checked correlations to identify overlapping information. Strong correlation is a warning about redundancy, not an automatic reason to delete a feature.

Market breadth and average market return overlap, as do some momentum indicators. I kept the selected predictors to compare the same inputs in both models; logistic regression uses regularization. Correlation here is between inputs, not evidence that they predict tomorrow.

4. Train/test split and classification

This is classification, not prediction of an exact price. I used logistic regression first and a random forest as one nonlinear comparison. I kept the existing model settings rather than continuing to tune them.

I retained the 80/20 random split from class, then checked a date-based split: train before 2017; test from 2017 onward. All training labels must also fall before the cutoff. This is an additional robustness check, not a completely untouched test, since I already explored this dataset. Same-day observations can share market conditions, making a random split optimistic for forecasting. Method reference: scikit-learn.

6. What I learned

The random forest reached 63.91% accuracy on the random split, but 51.39% on later dates, below the 52.73% majority baseline. The larger random-split score did not carry over to the forecasting check. The result is consistent with an optimistic random split, but this comparison alone does not explain the entire drop.

I learned how to turn raw prices into features and compare models against a simple baseline. The features described historical trading patterns, but this experiment did not establish a useful next-day forecasting advantage. I would not present it as a profitable trading model. The data covers one historical period, the stock list is not a historical membership record, and corporate actions were not independently verified.
